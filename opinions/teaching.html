Opinions · Teaching Philosophy

Teaching Philosophy

My goal in teaching is to cultivate independent thinkers.

Why teach physics

I think of physics as the study of how nature works at the most fundamental level. But teaching physics is more than teaching its theories and their applications. By studying physics, students learn to extract, condense, express and apply information about the world, both nature and human society. In an information age, where unreliable information is everywhere, that ability and critical thinking matter more than ever. So I aim to teach skills that transfer, to physics majors and others alike, including the mathematics and computing that physics needs.

I used to think teaching was about passing on knowledge. I now think it is about helping students construct their own, so my job is to make each student's learning as good as it can be, not to show what I know. College teaching should not be a selection process: students will face selection soon enough, and my job is to help every one of them as far as I can.

Lecturing

Multimedia has given students many ways to learn, but I still see lecturing as indispensable, as long as students are not passive receivers. I plan each course by backward design: I set the course goals, break them into learning outcomes, and plan class activities to reach them. The subjects I teach usually move from the concrete to the abstract, and that transition is where independent thinking lies, so I often make it the learning outcome of a lesson. In an introduction to probability, for example, I emphasize turning real-world problems into mathematical language.

In class, I often guide students with questions. In my Python course, I asked students to explain example code in their own words, then followed up with questions that helped them recall what they knew and find the right path. When they tackled the exercises on their own, they were never stuck at the start, and could always write down what they thought, which gave me something to respond to.

Feedback

As experts, we find it hard to imagine what novices do not know: the curse of knowledge, or expert blind spot. My remedy is feedback that is specific and constructive. When I graded graduate quantum mechanics, I gave each student detailed comments, and after each assignment I wrote to the whole class about recurring errors and how to fix them.

Independent thinkers should also be able to judge their own progress. I plan to use frequent short assessments, so students can check how they are doing against my feedback. They learn not only the material but how to learn, which makes them more confident and more willing to take part in class.

Students also come with very different backgrounds. I see that diversity as an asset, since every student brings something to the class, and I plan to meet it with short surveys at the start and support at more than one level.

Mentoring

Mentoring research is part of my teaching too, and it is hardest with undergraduates and new graduate students, who are new to research. In research, everyone faces the unknown, and even the mentor does not have all the answers. So I explain what research is like and help students set reasonable expectations, because frustration is often the biggest barrier for beginners. And however busy I am, I reply to beginners' questions promptly. My aim is for students to move from using code to building their own research tools.

My own path, from French literature through computer science to astrophysics, makes me especially glad to work with students whose backgrounds are not the usual ones.

Growing as a teacher

Higher education keeps changing with society and technology, and so does my teaching. I keep learning teaching methods, follow new educational technology, and ask colleagues for feedback. From time to time I sit in on classes in other disciplines, to remember what it is like to be a novice and to learn from other perspectives. A teacher is always a learner of teaching, and I try to keep a student's mindset.

Books that shaped my teaching:

  • Susan A. Ambrose et al., How Learning Works: Seven Research-Based Principles for Smart Teaching (2010)
  • Ken Bain, What the Best College Teachers Do (2004)
  • Grant Wiggins and Jay McTighe, Understanding by Design (2nd ed., 2005)
  • James M. Lang, Cheating Lessons: Learning from Academic Dishonesty (2013)
  • John C. Bean, Engaging Ideas (2nd ed., 2011)
  • Stephen D. Brookfield, Becoming a Critically Reflective Teacher (2017)

Plans and outreach

At MIDAS, I hope to run short workshops for students and researchers on high-performance Python, Git workflows and good practice in machine learning, and to mentor students working where a domain science meets AI. I see well-documented open-source software as a kind of outreach, because it lets researchers anywhere take part in large missions, and I look forward to public nights at Michigan's observatories and to Saturday Morning Physics.
