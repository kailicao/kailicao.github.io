Stars · Stellar Modeling

Stellar Modeling

Testing stellar evolution models against thousands of observed red giant stars.

In brief

Stars are the clocks and scales of the Universe: their ages date the history of the Milky Way, and their masses and sizes calibrate much of astronomy. Both depend on models of stellar structure and evolution. The Sun has been fusing hydrogen into helium for about 5 billion years; in another 5 billion, it will run out of hydrogen in its core and become a red giant, cooler but far more luminous. Large, homogeneous data sets now allow us to test the models of this phase in much more detail than before.

Two features of red giants are especially telling. In the first dredge-up, the deepening convective envelope brings nuclear-processed material to the surface, changing its chemical composition. And as the hydrogen-burning shell moves outward and meets the convective envelope, the star briefly dims before brightening again, which piles stars up at the red giant branch bump.

What I have done

With Marc Pinsonneault, I compared stellar models computed with MESA against APOKASC-3, a joint spectroscopic and asteroseismic catalog of red giants in the Kepler fields (Cao and Pinsonneault 2026; code, MESA inlists). Both features show robust tensions. The observed change in [C/N] at the first dredge-up is smaller than predicted, by about 0.16 dex, and the observed bump sits about 0.15 dex higher in surface gravity than predicted. A proposed fix for the bump, envelope undershooting, would also destroy more lithium during the first dredge-up at higher metallicities, in tension with what GALAH observes, so we used lithium to challenge that fix, and we propose lithium as a sensitive test of both features.

Building on my experience with MESA, I have also helped develop the Yale Rotating Stellar Evolution Code (YREC), as a coauthor of its public data release.
