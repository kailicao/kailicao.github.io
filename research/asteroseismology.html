Stars · Asteroseismology

Asteroseismology

Stellar oscillations as precise measures of stellar masses and ages, from Kepler to Roman.

In brief

Red giants oscillate, and their oscillations reveal their interiors. The Fourier transform of a star's light curve gives its oscillation power spectrum, characterized by two numbers: the frequency of maximum power, νmax, and the large frequency spacing, Δν. Scaling relations turn these into the star's mass and radius, and so its age, for many thousands of stars at once. Stellar ages tell us about the past of the Milky Way, which is the subject of galactic archaeology.

The scaling relations are motivated by theory but need to be calibrated with data. For the most luminous giants, they show clear systematic offsets, which have to be corrected.

What I have done

In APOKASC-3, the corrections tied asteroseismic radii to radii measured by Gaia, and the whole difference was ascribed to νmax. With Marc Pinsonneault, I proposed an alternative: since the corrections for Δν, which come from models, can be imperfect too, an arguably better use of the Gaia radii is to correct the asteroseismic radii directly (Cao and Pinsonneault 2025). The new formulation reduces the systematics in two ways: different asteroseismic methods agree better, and the results depend much less on how mean density is inferred from the oscillation frequencies. In the α-rich population, it reduces the mismatch in median mass between the lower and upper red giant branch from 6.65% to 1.72%, and in median age from −21.8% to −9.6%.

I also contributed to the APOKASC-3 catalog, especially the uncertainties of its spectroscopic measurements, and to related studies of red giants. Roman's Galactic Bulge Time Domain Survey is designed mainly to find exoplanets, but it will also be a great source of asteroseismic data, with far more targets than previous missions, in the Galactic bulge and center rather than the solar neighborhood: one forecast suggests oscillations in about 185,000 red giants in the bulge. I am a member of the Roman Wide-Field Science Team for asteroseismology with that survey.

What comes next

DynFlux: Roman's detectors are very sensitive, and about half of the survey's asteroseismic targets are expected to be saturated. DynFlux aims at reliable time-domain photometry of saturated stars. I also plan to help characterize the evolved stars Roman observes.
